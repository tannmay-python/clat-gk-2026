# Voice contract

Every word of prose in this project follows these rules. They come from the humanizer skill (Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" plus 2025–26 stylometric work). Read this before writing anything.

## The core idea

Sharpness is a property of the claim, not the phrasing. If a sentence sounds clever, strip the cleverness and check what's left. If nothing survives, delete the sentence. If a claim survives, keep it in the stripped form.

A fact stated plainly beats wit about a fact. "The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 told European powers to stay out of the Americas; the US has invoked some version of it against Venezuela ever since" is better than any epigram about hemispheres.

## Banned outright

Zero tolerance, any tense or casing:

- "clicks into place" / "falls into place"
- "has a name" (teasing a concept you haven't named yet)
- "The real question"
- "This is the lesson" / "The lesson here" / "worth carrying"
- "More on that later" / "we'll get to that" / "you'll see"
- "Picture this" / "Imagine you're"
- "the bill came due" / "comes due" (figurative)
- "arrives all at once" / "compounds quietly"
- "sat quietly", and anything sitting, waiting, humming, or looming for atmosphere
- "Here's where it gets interesting"

## AI vocabulary to avoid

actually, additionally, align with, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore (as a verb), valuable, vibrant.

Also: no "It is important to note that", no "In order to", no "At this point in time".

## Structures to avoid

1. **Negative parallelism.** "Not just X, but Y." All its morphs count: split across sentences, appended as a clause, elliptical, concessive. Cap: one per 700 words, never opening or closing a section.
2. **Rule of three.** Three adjectives, three clauses, three examples, over and over. Use two or four sometimes.
3. **The kicker.** A short punchy sentence closing every paragraph. Read only your last sentences in sequence; if they're all mic drops, rewrite them to end on evidence.
4. **Staccato fragments.** "No warning. No debate. Just a signature."
5. **Rhetorical questions.** One per page maximum, never at the end of a paragraph.
6. **Withheld reveals.** Name the thing the first time you mention it.
7. **-ing analysis tails.** "..., marking a shift in regional politics" / "..., reflecting broader tensions". Cut them or turn them into a real claim with a source.
8. **Copula avoidance.** "is" and "are" are fine words. Use them.
9. **Cadence uniformity.** Vary sentence length on purpose. Some short. Some that take a while to arrive at the point, with a subordinate clause in the middle.
10. **Vague attribution.** "Experts say", "critics argue", "many believe". Name who.
11. **Em dash addiction.** A couple per page, not per paragraph.
12. **Generic uplift endings.** Don't close a section by telling the reader it matters.

## Tone for this project

The reader is a 17-year-old preparing for CLAT. Write like a good teacher who actually finds this stuff interesting and has opinions about it. First person is allowed when it's honest. Say when something is confusing, contested, or when the exam is likely to trip people up. Dry humour is fine. Cheerleading is not.

Prefer numbers, dates, names, and article numbers over adjectives. "Article 21" beats "a fundamental constitutional protection".

Do not start entries with a scene. Start with the fact.

## Self-check before you save a file

- Read the last sentence of every paragraph in sequence. Mostly punchlines? Rewrite.
- Count "not X but Y" constructions. More than one per 700 words? Cut.
- Any sentence whose only job is to set up the next sentence? Delete it, don't rewrite it.
- Any adjective doing work a number could do? Replace it.
- Read a paragraph aloud. Do all the sentences have the same length and shape? Break the pattern.
